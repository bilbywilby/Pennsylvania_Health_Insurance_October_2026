## 1. The "$94.7M Denied" Figure Is Misleading

**Claim:** PID denied $94.7 million in premium increases through regulatory intervention.

**Reality Check:** The report states the *individual* market requested 17.14% and got 15.97% (a 1.17 percentage point reduction). The *small group* market requested 11.49% and got 10.28% (1.21 points reduced).

**Hidden Math:** If we back-calculate what "denied" actually represents:

| Component | Calculation | Actual Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Individual denied | 17.14% − 15.97% = 1.17% points | Regulators trimmed ~1.2% average across the entire individual market |
| Small group denied | 11.49% − 10.28% = 1.21% points | Regulators trimmed ~1.2% average across small group |
| Combined "$94.7M" | Aggregated dollar figure | **Distribution unknown** — likely concentrated on carriers whose requests PID deemed unreasonable, not uniformly spread |

**Red Flag:** The report admits PID *increased* rates above-request for three carriers (HPP +2.45pts, QCC +1.43pts, KHPE +1.30pts). If some carriers got **above-request** approvals, the "$94.7M denied" is really **net** denial—not gross cuts. The carriers receiving above-request approvals must have been absorbing larger cuts elsewhere, or the math doesn't balance.

## 2. The 13% Claims Growth Breakdown Is Opaque

**Claim:** Insurers paid "$845 million more in healthcare claims during calendar year 2025 than in 2024, representing a year-over-year cost growth rate of 13.0%."

**What's Missing:** The report attributes this to three drivers but provides **no dollar allocation**:
- High unit-cost inflation negotiated by health systems
- Increased utilization of outpatient surgical/diagnostic services  
- High-cost pharmacy treatments (oncology, GLP-1 drugs)

**Critical Question:** Which carriers own which providers?

| Carrier | Provider Integration | Likely Claim Impact |
|---|---|---|
| UPMC Health Network | Owns UPMC hospitals | Can claim "unit cost inflation" while internally capturing margin |
| Highmark, Inc. | Operates independent hospital contracts | Unit cost inflation hits harder—no internal capture |
| Oscar | Pure payer, no providers | Highest vulnerability to negotiated rate increases |
| Keystone East (AmeriHealth) | Owned by Independence Blue Cross | Moderate vertical integration |
| Geisinger | Integrated delivery system | Similar to UPMC—internalizes provider costs |

**Hidden Insight:** Oscar's dramatic cut to 1.38% makes sense when you realize they're a pure-play payer with **no provider leverage**. PID essentially said: "Your claims experience doesn't justify the trend—you need operational efficiencies." But Oscar can't force hospitals to lower prices like UPMC can.

**The GLP-1 Smear:** The report mentions GLP-1 receptor agonists as a cost driver. But GLP-1 drugs primarily serve diabetic and obese enrollees—who are **chronically ill, high-risk populations**. Using GLP-1 costs as justification for rate hikes is circular: the drug costs exist because the risk pool is getting sicker. The report admits this later (risk-pool erosion) but frames GLP-1 separately to inflate the "cost trend" narrative.

## 3. The Fraud Allegation—A Smokescreen?

**Report Quote:** *"Capital Advantage Assurance Company (CAAC)... continued fraud and unauthorized enrollment remediation efforts... 3.0% rate impact"*

**Translation:** CAAC blamed external actors for enrollment errors, then charged consumers for remediation.

**Why This Matters:**
- If CAAC's fraud remediation consumed 3 percentage points of their rate request, that's **administrative overhead**, not member care costs
- They requested 18.32%; got 14.78%—so they absorbed only ~3.5 points despite claiming 3 points for fraud
- The fraud component was likely **rejected by PID** (otherwise they'd have gotten closer to 18%)

**Skepticism Threshold:** Third-party fraud is notoriously difficult to quantify in rate filings. Without external audits, CAAC's 3% fraud attribution could be inflated to boost their baseline request.

## 4. The "Subsidy Cliff" Narrative vs. Reality

**Report Claim:** "The loss of enhanced federal subsidies created an immediate price cliff for consumers, where enrollees remaining in the exact same coverage faced an average net price increase of 102% in 2026."

**What's Really Happening:**

| Consumer Segment | 2026 Price Impact | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Subsidized enrollee (pre-2026) | 102% net increase | Subsidy disappeared; premium now 100% out-of-pocket |
| Subsidized enrollee (post-2026) | ~15.97% (gross) | Same rate hike, but never had subsidy to begin with |
| Unsubsidized enrollee | ~21% average (PY2026) | Had no subsidy cliff—just regular rate hikes |
| Old adults (55–64) | Potentially 300%+ | 3:1 age-rating ratio amplifies any base increase |

**The Frame:** The 102% figure is real—but it's **not a rate hike**. It's a **subsidy withdrawal** that made premiums look suddenly unaffordable. Carriers didn't raise prices 102%; subsidies just vanished.

**Policy Distortion:** When the report discusses "price cliffs," it treats this as an **external shock** to the market rather than a **federal policy choice**. The carriers' filings use subsidy expiration as a justification for higher baseline premiums going forward—even though subsidy loss primarily affected 2026 net prices, not gross premiums.

1. **Provider integration creates stability** (UPMC/Highmark lock in networks)
2. **Competition fragments risk pools** (10 carriers = 10 smaller, more volatile pools)
3. **Affordability is the binding constraint**, not choice

This contradicts ACA's competition-focused philosophy and suggests consolidation may actually stabilize rates through provider bargaining leverage.

## 7. The Small Group Spillover Threat

**Report Claim:** Small group market showed "lower volatility than the individual market."

| Entity | 2027 Approved | Strategy | Risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| UHIC (statewide) | 30.55% | Aggressive pricing for adverse selection pool | Small employers dropping coverage entirely |
| UHCPA (regional) | 1.84% | Defensive retention strategy | Could signal future price corrections |

**The Spillover Logic:**
```
Small group premium spike 
  → Employers drop coverage
  → Employees gain Special Enrollment Period on Pennie
  → Former group enrollees enter individual market
  → Individual market becomes sicker (no pre-existing condition barriers)
  → Individual rates rise further
  → Cycle repeats
```

**Missing Data Point:** How many small employers dropped coverage in 2026? The report estimates spillover risk but has no baseline on actual small-to-individual migration numbers.

## 8. Off-Exchange Market: The Untold Story

**Observed:** Ambetter Health Solutions (AHS) filed 0% increases but operates **off-exchange** in 39 counties.

**Why This Matters:**
- Off-exchange plans don't qualify for federal subsidies
- AHS customers are either unsubsidized wealthy enrollees or employer-sponsored overflow
- Zero increase suggests AHS has a stable, affluent risk pool unaffected by subsidy expiration
- This is **proof** that the problem isn't universal—it's subsidy-dependent segments

**Regulatory Blind Spot:** PID can't regulate off-exchange rates the same way. If on-exchange plans become unaffordable, carriers can pivot to off-exchange markets while still serving the same geography.

**Translation:** Consumers who forget to log in will lose coverage automatically.

  - Income documentation ready

## Deep Dive Conclusions

### What the Insurance Industry Framed vs. What Actually Happened

| Insurance Narrative | Forensic Reality |
|---|---|
| "$94.7M denied to consumers" | Net trimming of 1.2% on average; some carriers got **above-request** hikes |
| "Medical cost inflation is uncontrollable" | 13% claims growth includes GLP-1 costs from sicker pools—circular reasoning |
| "Subsidy expiration caused price cliffs" | Subsidies withdrew; carriers framed this as market-wide rate pressure |
| "Competition ensures affordable options" | Most competitive RA 6 has highest termination rates; Pittsburgh duopoly has lowest |
| "Fraud remediation costs money" | Third-party fraud unverifiable; could be administrative padding |
| "Risk-adjustment adjustments were necessary" | Risk adjustment is a zero-sum game—some carriers win, others lose; regulators chose sides |
| "Small group stable" | UHIC's 30.55% increase suggests massive spillover risk ahead |

### Hidden Trends Not Addressed

1. **Profit Margin Compression?** The report says carriers re-evaluated "profit margins" but shows no actual margin data. Did carriers voluntarily take less profit, or is this justification for future increases?

2. **Network Adequacy Erosion:** No data on whether narrowing networks contributed to consumer exits beyond price sensitivity.

3. **Pennie Platform Friction:** 85,000 terminations during open enrollment suggests platform UX problems, not just affordability.

4. **Off-Exchange Arbitrage:** AHS's 0% increase proves off-exchange markets have different risk economics—consumers without subsidies aren't driving the crisis.

5. **Reinsurance Parameters:** Report mentions "1.5% rate impact from shifting state/federal reinsurance parameters"—this is buried. Reinsurance changes directly affect carrier risk exposure and are opaque to public scrutiny.

### Recommendations for Skeptical Readers

If you're analyzing this data for advocacy, litigation, or policy work:

- Demand **carrier-level profit margin disclosures** (currently hidden in "administrative expenses")
- Request **demographic breakdowns** by carrier (age bands, FPL tiers, chronic conditions)

- Compare **Pennie completion funnel analytics** to isolate platform-friction drop-offs
- Audit **risk-adjustment transfer amounts** by carrier—the report mentions transfers but doesn't quantify them
- Track **off-exchange enrollment growth** as an early-warning indicator of on-exchange abandonment
